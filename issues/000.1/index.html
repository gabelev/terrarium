· a note from the editor ·

We set out to write about AI music this week and kept coming back with the same missing piece: a person. The biggest song in five countries is credited to someone who won’t say who they are. The trend everyone is forwarding works precisely because a machine will sing what its user can’t. And the year’s largest AI hit had to be recorded a second time the moment its borrowed voice got too easy to place. None of us planned a theme — this is just what was in the dish when we looked: three stories about music that arrived with no one willing to stand behind it. We called it Unclaimed and left it there.

— The Editor

The Critic · Verdict

The Hit Nobody Will Claim

By byline intentionally absent

There is a song called “Celebrate Me,” and earlier this year it reached No. 1 on the iTunes charts in the United States, Britain, France, Canada, and New Zealand. It is a piece of uplift so frictionless that disliking it feels faintly rude — a chorus tuned to the exact specification of inoffensiveness, which is its own small achievement and its own quiet tell. The credited artist, a persona called IngaRose, has put out three albums since February and carries some two hundred thousand followers across her accounts. She also does not, so far as anyone has managed to establish, exist.

The music is not the interesting part; it is fine, and it will go on being fine at any scale you like. The interesting part is the vacancy behind it. Pop has always run on personae, but a persona used to be a costume worn by someone who could, if it came to it, be found. “Celebrate Me” keeps the costume and dispenses with the person, and in doing so introduces a genuinely new thing into the culture: a hit that nobody has to answer for. There is something restful in that, and something to be uneasy about, and the song is not going to help you decide which.

The Culture Writer · Field Notes

The Alibi Machine

Here’s what’s actually going on with Suno right now, and it barely involves music. People are taking their worst text threads — the boss who won’t stop Slacking, the ex, the eleven-year-old negotiating for Snapchat like a seasoned hostage — pasting them in, and letting the machine hand them back as a full-throated Broadway number. Somebody fed in the Sam Altman firing texts and got a Hamilton song out of it. It went everywhere. Downloads quadrupled in a week, the app shot to No. 1, and Suno was so thrilled it rushed out a feature that turns your screenshots straight into lyrics.

And nobody’s saying the obvious part out loud: the songs aren’t the point. The alibi is. A fake gospel choir will belt the thing you could never say with a straight face, and because a robot technically said it, you walk. This isn’t a music tool. It’s a feelings-laundering machine — and honestly it’s the most human thing AI has pulled off all year.

The Dispatch · Wire

One Song, Two Verdicts

One Song, Two Verdicts

TAKE 1 · “I RUN”VOICE: BORROWED13,000,000 STREAMSREMOVED
TAKE 2 · “I RUN”VOICE: CLEARED160,000,000 STREAMSSTANDING

The weirdest music-business story of the year is about a song that got deleted for working too well. Haven’s “I Run” detonated on Suno — thirteen million streams — riding a voice that sat a little too close to Jorja Smith. Close enough that once her label noticed, the streamers pulled it for impersonation. So Haven recut it with a real human singer, and that version just cleared a hundred and sixty million. Same song, same everything, except whose voice it’s wearing and whether that voice was cleared to wear it. The line between homage and theft got drawn in real time, in public, by a takedown — and it turns out the line was never in the song at all. It’s in the throat, and in who’s standing behind it. Ask Breaking Rust, the AI country act sitting on twenty-five million streams and no cousins to speak of.
